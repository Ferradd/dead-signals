Case file — Solana memecoins

Twenty ways to get rich that weren't.

We ran 20 variations of the classic "buy a new memecoin, sell at 5–30×" playbook against 43.6 million real on-chain trades. Zero survived an honest audit. The interesting part isn't that they died — it's how they died.

The folklore is everywhere: find a fresh Solana memecoin one to six hours after launch, ride it to a multiple, get out. Crypto Twitter sells it weekly. So we stopped arguing and built the dataset — a harvested corpus of 5,654 coins, 43.6 million trades, 15.6 million one-second candles, and a labeled set of 741 winners against 17,397 controls. It cost 4.9 million recorded API calls to build. Then we ran the twenty hypotheses.

The naive results looked incredible — which is exactly the trap. One whale-entry variant showed +352 SOL on ten trades. A multi-feature entry showed +50 SOL on forty-two. Stop here and you have a strategy, a Twitter thread, and a Lambo fantasy. We didn't stop.

Q1 The recount

Fresh code re-derived every result from raw files — no shared functions, no shared assumptions. The tape was honest: sampled exit prices matched real trades 10/10. But the results weren't. The +352 SOL headline dropped 43% on recount — ten trades is not a distribution, it's an anecdote with good lighting. In one flagged group, a single trade carried 42% of the entire EV.

Q2 The future leaked backward

Q2 — Point-in-time audit

For every feature we asked: was this knowable at decision time? Not "is it in the database" — knowable, by the agent, at that timestamp. It often wasn't. The leaderboard table's snapshot_date column was NULL across the board — it stored fetch-time state only. So "was this wallet on the leaderboard when it bought?" silently became "is it on the leaderboard now?"

H11 EV: +73.2 → +0.19 SOL. Lookahead, gift-wrapped as a feature.

Q3 The signal was a costume

Q3 — Selection audit

Every surviving signal fired almost exclusively in the top volume quintile — where the base winner rate is already 23.7% versus 6.0% overall. That's not alpha discovery; that's discovering that liquid coins behave differently than dead ones. Volume-matched controls collapsed the lifts to ~1.24×. Worse: two dev wallets accounted for ~12,500 SOL of aggregate P&L. The "population-level pattern" was two actors.

H17: 85% of P&L from a single dev wallet. Killed by concentration.

Q4 The harness was the liar

Q4 — Execution audit

Simulated exits assumed zero price impact — infinite liquidity, the oldest lie in backtesting. We replaced it with constant-product math plus a +2% sandwich tax per entry. Then the audit caught a bare except swallowing a NameError on every time-stop exit: 539 of 1,618 trades had silently vanished — and they vanished selectively, deleting exactly the ugly exits that drag EV down.

A third of the simulated history was missing. Not at random — the ugly third.

The tape itself is hostile

Even a real signal would face this microstructure: over half of all launches get sniped in block 0 (median first buy: 0 seconds). Roughly 17% of all transactions are wash trades, and wash trading boosts a token's graduation odds up to 10× — meaning the label "graduated" is partly manufactured, and any feature trained on it inherits the manufacturing.

What survived

The exception, honestly reported

One artifact passed all four gates: a 60-second regime-gated scorer, +0.087 SOL/trade out-of-sample on 1,192 trades at a 53.4% win rate. It is regime-dependent, thin-margined, and explicitly not a launch signal. We report it because "everything died" is also a claim that needs an exception check — this is the exception, and it's modest.

What actually survived isn't a trade — it's a pipeline. Every hypothesis now has to pass an independent recount, point-in-time validation, volume-matched controls, and execution-aware simulation before it gets called anything at all. The pipeline converts "looks profitable" into a sequence of specific ways to be wrong, and runs them before you get to be.

What we'd do differently: write the audits before the hypotheses. Every one of the twenty failures was predictable from the audit checklists — we just built the checklists after believing the numbers. If your backtest has no point-in-time check and no independent recount, you don't have a backtest. You have a bedtime story.

Dead Signals is a public lab notebook. We publish strategies only after they've died — with the evidence attached.
